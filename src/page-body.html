How to read

The board reads the economy the way a cycle-tracking app reads a body: one wheel for the cycle, a temperature chart, and a handful of vital signs. Every reading on it has an (i) with the full explanation and its source; this page is the short version.

The wheel

One turn of the dial is one market cycle. The outer ring is split one shape per season — each runs from the quarter a season began to the quarter it ended — in the temperature's colours: periwinkle below the 1–3% range, orange above it — deep periwinkle for Winter (below), light periwinkle for Spring (below or within; deflation or reflation), light yellow-orange for Autumn (within or above; disinflation or stagflation), orange for Summer (above). Tap any quarter to read the season the model computed for it.

The band inside the ring is the stock market, one segment per year — teal for a year the S&P 500 closed up, red for a year it closed down, lighter for the year in progress. The ringed dot on the band marks the peak year — the cycle's most profitable year, the one with the best S&P 500 return. The centre names today's season; tap This season for the reasoning behind it.

Press and hold the year badge and drag round the ring to move between quarters — it stays where you leave it, and dragging it back past the last quarter, or tapping anywhere outside the dial, returns it to today. Tap any quarter on the ring to read it in the centre.

The six seasons

A season is computed from two readings, never set by hand. Growth is the direction of real GDP. Each quarter is measured against the same quarter a year earlier, and the season reads the trend through the last six of those readings — a year and a half of them: rising is expansion, falling is contraction, and a flat stretch continues whichever came before it. Temperature is where inflation sits against a 1–3% band — hot above it, cold below it, warm within it — and which way it is heading over the last twelve months.

In expansion, hot prices make Summer — inflation; otherwise the direction decides — heating is Spring — reflation, cooling is Spring — deflation. In contraction, cold prices make Winter — deflation; otherwise cooling is Autumn — disinflation and heating or steady is Autumn — stagflation.

The 1–3% band is the board's own fixed tolerance around the Fed's 2% point objective, read on CPI. The full rule, with today's row marked, is on the Content tab under The Season Model.

Temperature and Growth

Temperature is a line through the months of the cycle — CPI, year over year — red where it runs above the band, teal inside it, blue below, with the 2% target drawn through it. Tap either chart to read a month on both at once; tap again, or anywhere else, to dismiss.

Growth sits directly beneath on the same axis: a line through the quarters of real GDP growth, teal above zero and red below, the dashed line its average over the closed years; the read-out at the end gives the latest quarter and whether the trend of the last eight quarters is rising or falling — the reading the season’s growth direction comes from.

Sentiment and rates

Market sentiment has a drawer of its own, Sentiment, carrying the fear curve — the shape of expected volatility — and the market it is read beside: the VIX. The Fed funds rate sits where it belongs, inside Temperature under the Lagging signs: that card is inflation and monetary policy together, and the rate is the lever pulled after her temperature, not ahead of it.

Her energy is a category of its own, Energy: the federal debt, the interest payments and the federal budget, each read on its own page, beside the households and the labour market. In the body, stress and energy are the same system — a shock diverts energy from digestion, growth and repair to the muscles and the brain, and stress that never lifts spends the reserve until fatigue is what remains. Those markers are structural, so they move slowly, by design; the fast reads are Feeling and Pulse.

The tabs

Current Cycle is the view above, for the cycle under way. Calendar lists the five named cycles since 1991; tap one to see it in the same view, computed from the same series. Below the wheel, the same tab carries today's readings — pressure and its recession record, power, sentiment and valuations, and the real-time and lagging signs — as drawers that open and close. Cycle Analysis lays every cycle flat, its seasons in order in the dial's colours, and says what usually comes after the season we are in. Search lists every reading by category — Weather, Mood, Circulation and Energy — with a filter by timing; the season model and the manuscript's framework table are under About Gyneconomy.

About Gyneconomy

Mrs. Market is a book about the economy for people who are neither economists nor doctors. It reads economic cycles through the one cycle most of us already understand from the inside: the menstrual cycle.

The idea

The body is the economy. Markets are its nervous system; hormones and prices are its signals; growth and inflation are its energy budget; interest rates are the lever that cools it after it has already run hot. Read this way, the phases of an economic cycle stop being abstractions and become seasons — Spring, Summer, Autumn and Winter — each with its own temperature, its own mood, and its own right action.

The framework, which the book calls Gyneconomy™, borrows its discipline from two places: Howard Marks's Mastering the Market Cycle for the economics, and Alexandra Pope and Sjanie Hugo Wurlitzer's Wild Power for the cycle. Every economic claim in it is grounded in verified biological research before it is allowed on the page.

This board

The board is the book's companion: the same framework, run on real data. It computes the current season from growth and inflation exactly as the book defines it, shows every past cycle since 1991 the same way, and keeps the vital signs the book teaches you to watch. Every figure is compiled by hand from primary sources and fixed at publish time — nothing on it updates on its own.
